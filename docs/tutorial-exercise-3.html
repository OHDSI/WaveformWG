OHDSI Waveform WG

Exercise 3: Derive features

Goal

Extract heart rate and the QT interval from the signal, store them as waveform_feature rows linked to MEASUREMENT rows, and check the result against the answer key.

Background

Analysts rarely work with samples. They work with features: numbers derived from a window of signal. For this tutorial the feature of interest is the QTc: the time from the start of the QRS complex to the end of the T wave (QT), corrected for heart rate because QT shortens when the heart beats faster. Two common corrections are

  • Bazett: QTcB = QT / sqrt(RR)
  • Fridericia: QTcF = QT / RR^(1/3)

with QT and RR in the same unit (seconds in the formulas, reported in ms).

In the Waveform Extension a derived value takes two forms. The MEASUREMENT row is the standard OMOP fact that cohort and covariate tooling understands. The waveform_feature row is the provenance: which occurrence, file and channel it came from, which algorithm produced it, and over which time window. They are linked by measurement_id.

Learning Outcomes

  1. Run an interval detector on a WFDB signal and read its output
  2. Quantify detector error against a known truth, separately for clean and noisy recordings
  3. Store features with provenance in waveform_feature and MEASUREMENT
  4. Use a signal-quality flag to exclude recordings
  5. Explain why Bazett and Fridericia give different answers at different heart rates

Materials

  • The detector: src/qtcdetect.py. It filters lead II, finds R peaks, finds QRS onset from the steepest slope, and calls the T-wave end the point where the smoothed signal returns to within 5% of the T-wave height. It is a teaching detector, not a clinical one.
  • Feature writer: src/extract_features.py
  • Reference solution: tutorial/solutions/ex3_feature_extraction.py
  • Answer key: data/answer_key/records_truth.csv

Steps

3.1 One record by hand

import sys, wfdb, pandas as pd
sys.path.insert(0, "src")
from qtcdetect import analyze

rec = wfdb.rdrecord("data/wfdb/A0000001")
f = analyze(rec.p_signal[:, rec.sig_name.index("II")], rec.fs)
truth = pd.read_csv("data/answer_key/records_truth.csv")
print(f)
print(truth[truth.accession == "A0000001"].T)

You should get a heart rate of 69.9 bpm (exactly the truth), a QT of about 368 ms against a true 373 ms, and a QTcF of about 389 ms against a true 393 ms.

Question 3.1. The detector’s QT is a few milliseconds short of the truth. Why would a threshold-based T-end detector be biased in that direction?

Answer The T wave tails off slowly towards the baseline. A detector that declares the end when the signal first falls within 5% of the T-wave height stops slightly before the true end, so QT is underestimated. Real tangent-method and threshold detectors have their own known biases too, which is why QT values from different devices and algorithms are not interchangeable. That is the reason waveform_feature carries the algorithm.

3.2 All 542 records against the truth

import numpy as np
rows = [analyze(wfdb.rdrecord(f"data/wfdb/{a}").p_signal[:, 1], 250) for a in truth.accession]
est = pd.concat([truth, pd.DataFrame(rows)], axis=1)
est["err_qtcf"] = est.qtcf_ms - est.true_qtcf_ms
print(est.groupby(est.low_quality.astype(int)).err_qtcf.agg(["count", "mean", "std", lambda x: x.abs().max()]))

You should see about this:

Question 3.2. A systematic error of about -3.5 ms and a random error of about 2 ms. Which of the two matters more when you compare two groups?

Answer For a difference between groups, a systematic bias that is the same in both groups cancels. The random error does not cancel: it adds noise and widens confidence intervals, but if it is unrelated to treatment it does not bias the comparison. The thing to fear is differential error, for example a device used only in the treated group, or lower signal quality in one arm. Exercise 4 shows the effect of measuring QTc with and without the noisy records.

3.3 The quality flag

The detector reports noise_mv, the SD of the 60-100 Hz band (far from the 50 Hz powerline), and flags records above 0.05 mV. Compare the flag with the answer key’s noisy column.

print(pd.crosstab(est.noisy, est.low_quality))

Question 3.3. The flag agrees with the truth for every record. Why would you not expect that on real data, and what does that mean for how you would choose the threshold?

Answer Here clean and noisy records are two well-separated populations (about 0.004 mV versus 0.10 mV), so any threshold between them works. Real noise is a continuum and some artifacts (a few bad beats, baseline jumps) do not show in a band-power measure. In practice you choose a threshold by looking at the distribution, check a sample of records by eye on both sides of it, and treat the threshold as an analysis parameter whose effect you report, which is exactly what Exercise 4 does with a sensitivity analysis.

3.4 Store the features

src/extract_features.py does the writing. For each ECG and each feature it adds

  • a MEASUREMENT row: measurement_concept_id (for example QTc interval, Fridericia), value_as_number, unit, and the visit
  • a waveform_feature row: the occurrence, the registry row of the .dat file, the lead II channel metadata row, measurement_id, algorithm_concept_id, the time window, and, when the detector flagged the record, value_as_concept_id = Low signal quality

Look at the output for one ECG:

SELECT wf.waveform_feature_id, m.measurement_source_value, wf.value_as_number, wf.unit_source_value,
       wf.algorithm_source_value, wf.value_as_concept_id
FROM waveform_feature wf JOIN measurement m ON m.measurement_id = wf.measurement_id
WHERE wf.waveform_occurrence_id = 1;

Question 3.4. The same QT value could be stored as a MEASUREMENT row only. What would you lose?

Answer Provenance. From the MEASUREMENT row alone you cannot tell which file, which lead or which algorithm produced the number, so you cannot re-derive it when a better algorithm arrives, audit an outlier by looking at the signal, or restrict an analysis to one algorithm. Conversely, with only the waveform_feature row, standard OHDSI tooling that looks in MEASUREMENT would not see the value. The extension deliberately keeps both and links them.

3.5 Bazett versus Fridericia

In this dataset the truth follows Fridericia. Check how each correction’s error depends on heart rate:

ok = est[~est.low_quality]
print(np.corrcoef(ok.hr_bpm, ok.qtcb_ms - ok.true_qtcf_ms)[0, 1],
      np.corrcoef(ok.hr_bpm, ok.qtcf_ms - ok.true_qtcf_ms)[0, 1])

The correlations are about 0.97 for Bazett and 0.34 for Fridericia.

Question 3.5. Why is a correction that depends on heart rate a threat in a drug comparison, even though both arms use the same formula?

Answer If heart rate differs between the groups, or is related to who gets the drug (through age, heart failure or the indication), a formula that over-corrects at fast rates produces a QTc difference that is partly a heart rate difference. Adjusting for heart rate helps, but choosing the formula before looking at results, and showing results under both, is better practice. Exercise 4 compares both outcomes.
